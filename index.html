Core Message

Academic integrity in the AI age is not simply about banning or allowing tools. It is about explicitly teaching students how to use AI responsibly, verify information, document support, cite when required, and protect the originality of their own thinking.

Why This Matters

The case for explicit AI and academic integrity teaching

Students are already encountering AI tools. Academic integrity expectations cannot remain implicit.

Do Not Assume — Teach Explicitly

Students need direct instruction on when AI is allowed, when it must be disclosed, when it becomes misconduct, how to verify AI-generated information, how to cite and document support, and how to explain what thinking is their own.

1

Students are already using AI

The question is not whether they have access, but whether they have been taught to use it responsibly.

2

Expectations cannot be implicit

Many students do not know the difference between support, over-reliance, and misconduct.

3

Process evidence matters more than ever

Notes, drafts, sources, and decisions are the most meaningful evidence of student thinking.

4

Trust and transparency

The goal is to build trust, transparency, ethical judgement, and student ownership — not surveillance.

5

AI literacy starts with information literacy

AI literacy must be taught alongside research skills, citation, paraphrasing, source evaluation, and reflection.

6

Detection alone is not enough

AI detection tools should not be the main foundation of academic integrity decisions.

The academic integrity shift

Moving away from
  • Hidden tool use
  • Final-product-only assessment
  • Fear-based detection
  • Unclear, implicit rules
  • AI detection as primary evidence
Moving towards
  • Visible process and transparent documentation
  • Source verification and student reflection
  • Teacher–student dialogue
  • Professional authenticity review
  • Explicit teaching and shared expectations

What the IB requires when AI is used

The IB does not ban AI tools, but it is explicit about how AI use must be acknowledged. The summary below draws on Appendix 6; use it alongside the current policy and task-specific requirements.

The IB does not regard AI-produced work as the student's own

Any AI-generated text, image, or graph included in a piece of work must be treated like any other quoted or sourced material. It must be credited in the body of the text and referenced in the bibliography. Failing to do so misrepresents the content as the student's own — a form of academic misconduct.

How students must reference AI
  • If a student uses text or any other product from an AI tool — by copying, paraphrasing, or modifying an image — they must clearly reference the AI tool in the body of their work and in the bibliography.
  • Clearly mark verbatim borrowed wording with quotation marks or block quotation formatting, as appropriate, and provide a citation. Paraphrases still need citations but are not direct quotations. Follow the school’s citation style for the form of the citation itself.
  • The citation must include the prompt given to the AI tool and the date the content was generated.
Language and translation rules
  • The current policy permits basic spelling and non-generative grammar checks that preserve meaning and do not supply content. This is not a blanket permission to use generative AI to rewrite assessed work.
  • Language acquisition has stricter restrictions because sentence construction is assessed. Check the subject rules and any approved access arrangements before using language-support tools.
  • For IB submission, students may not write an essay in one language and use AI to translate it into another. This applies beyond language-acquisition subjects.

What the IB expects before a teacher authenticates work as the student's own

1

Developed over time

The teacher has seen the student develop the work over a period — IB coursework is not designed to be completed in a single evening.

2

Student can explain it

The student can explain their work sufficiently to give confidence that they created it.

3

Clear about sourcing

The student is clear when quoting others' ideas and when claiming an idea or conclusion as their own.

4

Quality matches the student

The final work is in line with what the teacher would expect that student to produce.

Citing generative AI in MLA style

The MLA recommends treating an AI tool as a source, not an author. Cite it whenever you paraphrase, quote, or incorporate AI-created content; acknowledge functional uses such as editing or translating; and always vet any secondary sources the AI cites.

Anatomy of an MLA AI citation

Title of source
Describe what was generated, or the prompt
Title of container
The AI tool, e.g. ChatGPT (italicised)
Version
The model name or number, as specifically as available
Publisher
The company that made the tool
Date
When the content was generated
Location
A stable, shareable link to the generated content; use the tool’s general URL only if unavailable

While the green light in The Great Gatsby might be said to symbolise optimism, the unattainability of the American dream, greed, and covetousness ("Describe the symbolism"), arguably the most important — the one that ties them together — is greed.

Vet the sources AI cites

Generative AI tools may invent plausible-looking citations using real journal names or real scholars who never wrote the cited work. If you quote an AI summary that lists sources, consult those sources yourself before relying on them — and if you cannot verify a source, do not use it.

Supporting the Process

Supporting students through the writing process

Simple ways to be alongside students as they work — shared documents, drafts, feedback and check-ins — so they get help when they need it and their genuine effort is seen and valued.

IB assessment boundaries

The routines below need adapting for formal coursework. The Extended Essay permits supervisor comments on one draft, without direct editing. Frequent check-ins are not permission for repeated draft correction or unrestricted peer feedback. Apply the relevant subject guide and assessment-session rules. EE guide, first assessment 2027, p. 104.

Support, not surveillance

When students build a piece over time in a shared space, you can be alongside them — giving feedback, catching confusion early, and reassuring them that their own effort is what counts. The goal is to help students do their best work honestly, not to watch over their shoulder. A happy side-effect: their genuine effort is visible, which quietly protects them if questions ever arise.

Why working in the open helps students

When the work develops in a shared space, you can spot who needs help early and offer it — a stronger source, a clearer plan, a word of encouragement — while there is still time to act.

A student who's thriving
  • Working steadily over days or weeks
  • Drafting, revising, and acting on feedback
  • Notes and research growing alongside the writing
  • Talks through their choices with confidence
A student who may need a nudge
  • Hasn't started drafting yet
  • Little has changed since the last check-in
  • A big jump in quality — check in to see how they're doing
  • Hasn't opened the doc in a while — a friendly reminder may help
A student who may be struggling
  • Nothing started close to the deadline
  • No drafts or notes to build from
  • Finds it hard to talk about their own work
  • Leaning on AI because they feel stuck — offer support

Tools that help you support the process

These are everyday tools for giving feedback, running check-ins, and helping students build good habits. Most schools use two or three together.

SP

SharePoint / OneDrive Shared Document

Microsoft 365

Students work in a shared Word document stored in a teacher-visible SharePoint or OneDrive folder. You can drop in to leave feedback and see how the work is developing through Version History.

How to set it up
Create a teacher-accessible folder or document for each student. Check permissions so classmates cannot view or edit individual assessed work unless collaboration is permitted. Students save their working document there from the start.
Ask students to use Track Changes while editing, so additions and deletions are clearly visible.
Record major milestones in a dated draft log or document comment (e.g. "First draft completed"). SharePoint check-in comments depend on the library workflow; they are not a universal save feature.
Open Version History from the document title or file menu. Review available versions and restore one if needed. Saves may be grouped; this is not a record of every keystroke. Comparison features vary between Word for the web and desktop Word.
Leave comments right in the document so students get feedback while they are drafting — not just at the end.
Version history makes it easy to see if someone is stuck early, so you can check in and offer help — a check-in, not a check-up.
GD

Google Docs Version & Edit History

Google Workspace

Google Docs keeps version history, but revisions may be grouped or merged. Editor access is required to view version history; Commenter access permits feedback but not history access. Teachers with Editor access should avoid changing the student’s assessed writing.

How to use it
Share the document with the teacher from the start. Students work in the shared doc — not a personal copy pasted in at the end.
Open File → Version history → See version history (Windows/ChromeOS: Ctrl+Alt+Shift+H; Mac: ⌘+Option+Shift+H). Review the available revisions and contributors.
Coach students as they draft using comments — suggest a stronger source, a clearer topic sentence, a missing citation — while there is still time to act on it.
For formative tasks where collaboration is permitted, peer comments can support revision. Do not apply this routine to individual assessed coursework unless its rules expressly allow it.
Ask students to use named versions (File → Version history → Name current version) to mark key milestones like "After first draft" or "Post feedback".
If something looks off, treat it as a reason to check in and offer help — a friendly conversation, never an accusation.
NT

NoodleTools Research Log & Notecards

NoodleTools

NoodleTools provides dated research records, including notecard creation and last-update information. Its recent notecard history covers a limited period; it is not a complete, immutable record of every research action.

How to use it
Have each student share their individual project with the teacher’s assignment inbox. Students add sources and notecards as they research and label later additions accurately.
Require students to create notecards that include: direct quote or paraphrase, the student's own annotation, and a connection to the argument.
Use light check-ins on the research trail to catch students who are stuck early — so you can help them find sources and get going.
Use NoodleTools' teacher dashboard to see who might need a nudge or a little extra help getting their research started.
Use NoodleTools’ AI citation option when citing generated content. Keep a separate AI-use log or clearly labelled note for purpose, prompts, decisions, and verification; “AI Support” is a suggested label, not a built-in source type.
NoodleTools timestamps reflect when students added notes to the platform — not when they did the underlying reading. Use in combination with oral check-ins about sources.
PP

Process Portfolio / Research Journal

School-designed

A structured process portfolio or research journal — whether digital (shared document) or on paper — asks students to record their thinking at each stage: question development, source notes, drafts, feedback received, and reflections on AI use.

What to include
Research question development log — early ideas, supervisor feedback, revisions, and final question with rationale.
Source log — each source read, with a short annotation of what it contributed to the student's thinking.
Draft record — retain your own earlier drafts and the feedback permitted for the task. Do not require multiple rounds of teacher or peer feedback on restricted assessments.
AI documentation section — for each AI tool used: date, purpose, output used, what was verified, and what was rejected.
Final reflection — what the student learned, what they would do differently, and what they identify as their own thinking.
Set up portfolios early. Existing notes and drafts can still be collected later, but label retrospective reflections honestly and do not present them as contemporaneous records.

How to respond to what you see

What you notice is a chance to help — not a verdict. Here is how to respond supportively.

If a student is on track

Acknowledge their effort and keep the feedback loop going. Steady, visible progress is exactly what you want to see — so tell them. A little encouragement goes a long way.

If a student seems to need a nudge

Check in early and kindly. Ask how it is going, offer a clear next step, point them to a source or a simpler plan. Stalls can have many causes, including access, confidence, workload, and wellbeing. Ask what support would help before drawing conclusions.

If a student is leaning on AI because they're stuck

Treat it as a teaching moment. Find out where they got stuck, reteach the skill, and give them a chance to show what they can do with support. Offer learning support while following the school’s academic-integrity procedures and applicable assessment deadlines whenever a concern arises.

Above all: this is about support, not catching students out

These tools exist to help students do their best work honestly and to make their genuine effort visible. When students feel supported and know their effort is valued, they are far more likely to engage with integrity — and the record of their work quietly protects them too.

IB AI Coursework Scenarios

Evaluating AI use in coursework — 13 scenarios

The goal is not to label all AI use as cheating. The goal is to ask whether AI helped the student learn, or whether it allowed them to pretend they had done thinking they had not actually done.

Guiding idea

Acceptable AI use supports learning. Unacceptable AI use replaces learning, hides support, or misrepresents student ownership.

Read the conditions with the label

Permission depends on the task. Disclosure does not authorise prohibited use. Research-question brainstorming requires supervisor involvement; language support must follow current policy. Read the teacher guidance before using a scenario as a rule.

1

AI summary of key points and suggested references

✓ Generally Acceptable

The student used AI to summarise key points and suggest references to investigate.

Why it matters

AI can support early research, but cannot replace source reading or student understanding.

Ask the student
  • Which suggested sources did you actually read?
  • What did you learn from them?
  • Can you explain the key points without relying on the AI output?
Evidence to request
  • Source notes
  • NoodleTools notecards
  • Verified references
  • AI disclosure statement
Documentation note

Disclose AI use for initial research support. Document which sources were actually read.

2

AI summary of alternative viewpoints

✓ Generally Acceptable

The student used AI to identify counter-positions and then explored them using independent sources.

Why it matters

AI can broaden perspective, but the student must still evaluate and understand the ideas independently.

Ask the student
  • Which viewpoints did AI suggest?
  • Which did you investigate further?
  • How did your own position change?
Evidence to request
  • Comparison notes
  • Source triangulation
  • NoodleTools source list
Documentation note

Document AI use for perspective-broadening. Show sources that confirm or challenge the viewpoints.

3

AI finds quotes — student copies without reading

✗ Not Acceptable

The student asked AI for quotes and copied them into the essay without reading the original sources.

Why it matters

The student is using AI to replace the research and thinking process. They cannot verify accuracy or context.

Ask the student
  • Did you read the original source?
  • Where exactly does this quote appear?
  • Why did you choose this quote?
Evidence to request
  • Original source
  • Accurate citation
  • Annotation of the source
  • NoodleTools notecard
Documentation note

All cited quotes must come from sources the student has read and understood.

4

AI develops the research question

✗ Not Acceptable

The student instructed AI to develop a research question grounded in an IB subject.

Why it matters

The final question must be developed by the student. After agreeing an area of interest with a supervisor, students may discuss AI-generated examples and adapt them through their own thinking.

Ask the student
  • How did you arrive at this question?
  • What personal interest led you here?
  • How did supervisor discussion shape the question?
Evidence to request
  • Brainstorming notes
  • Supervisor meeting notes
  • Earlier versions of the research question
Documentation note

Do not outsource the final research question. Supervisor-guided exploration of examples is distinct from asking AI to decide the question.

5

AI writes a model essay for the same task

⚠ Generally Not Acceptable — Review Context

The student asked AI to write an example of the same essay and used it as a model, even if translated or rewritten.

Why it matters

AI may have supplied the structure, argument, and ideas — meaning the student's work may not be genuinely their own.

Ask the student
  • Which parts came from your own thinking?
  • Can you explain the argument without the essay in front of you?
  • What sources did you personally read?
Evidence to request
  • Developmental drafts
  • Research trail
  • Source notes
  • Oral explanation / viva
Documentation note

Review understanding orally and follow the school’s procedure. The older scenario guidance allows a teaching response when understanding is demonstrated; never authenticate work that remains unoriginal or improperly attributed.

6

AI generates a paragraph as a model

◐ Context-Dependent

The student used AI to generate a paragraph as a model for their own writing.

Why it matters

The key question is whether AI replaced the student's thinking or simply provided an example of structure.

Ask the student
  • Did you use this for structure, wording, or ideas?
  • What did you change?
  • What is your own argument?
Evidence to request
  • Original AI output
  • Student draft
  • Revision notes
  • Final paragraph
Documentation note

Be especially careful with concluding paragraphs — these show the student's final thinking most clearly.

7

Student writes essay, then AI rewrites it

⚠ Generally Not Acceptable for IB Assessment

The student wrote an essay and then asked AI to rewrite it.

Why it matters

AI rewriting may alter the student's voice, coherence, argument, and expression.

Ask the student
  • Why did you ask AI to rewrite it?
  • What changed between versions?
  • Which version represents your own work?
Evidence to request
  • Original draft
  • AI-rewritten version
  • Version history
  • Student explanation
Documentation note

For IB assessment, mark the student's original work, not the AI-rewritten version.

8

AI translation for submission

✗ Not Acceptable (summative IB assessment)

The student wrote work in one language and used AI to translate it for submission.

Why it matters

Translation can change meaning, expression, and assessed language competence.

Ask the student
  • What language was the original written in?
  • Did the translation change the argument or wording?
Evidence to request
  • Original version
  • Translated version
  • Student explanation
  • Policy reference
Documentation note

Not acceptable for IB summative submissions, including non-language subjects. The restriction concerns the language in which the subject is studied, not only explicit language marks.

9

AI suggests grammar and sentence improvements

◐ Check Tool Type and Assessment Rules

The student used AI to improve grammar and sentence construction, without a wholesale rewrite.

Why it matters

Basic non-generative correction is different from a generative rewrite. Do not treat grammar assistance as automatically permitted for every assessment.

Ask the student
  • Did AI only correct grammar, or did it change meaning?
  • Can you show the before-and-after version?
Evidence to request
  • Original student writing
  • AI suggestions
  • Revised version
Documentation note

Apply current Appendix 6 and subject rules. Preserve the student’s meaning and content; language-acquisition restrictions and approved access arrangements also matter.

10

AI marks the work and gives feedback

◐ Context-Dependent

The student used AI to mark their work and provide feedback for improvement.

Why it matters

For IB assessment tasks, this may conflict with rules around teacher feedback. In non-assessment contexts, it may be useful.

Ask the student
  • Was this formative practice or formal coursework?
  • What feedback did AI give?
  • Did AI rewrite or reshape the work?
Evidence to request
  • AI feedback transcript
  • Student reflection
  • Draft before and after feedback
Documentation note

Do not assume AI marking or extra feedback is allowed on formal IB coursework. Check the subject’s feedback limits; formative practice can have different rules.

11

AI writes the student reflection

✗ Not Acceptable

The student asked AI to reflect on a topic or process and used that reflection unchanged in assessment.

Why it matters

Reflection must come from the student's own experience, thinking, learning, and judgement.

Ask the student
  • What did you personally learn?
  • What changed in your thinking?
  • What difficulties did you face?
Evidence to request
  • Personal process notes
  • Draft reflection
  • Oral explanation
Documentation note

Not acceptable. Reflection is one of the most personal forms of student voice. AI cannot do it for them.

12

Student hides AI use

✗ Not Acceptable

The student used AI but tried to hide it — even if the AI use itself might have been allowed.

Why it matters

Students must disclose support as required. Deliberately concealing relevant AI use undermines authentication; clarify the actual use and the communicated requirements before deciding next steps.

Ask the student
  • What AI support did you use?
  • Why was it not disclosed?
  • Which parts of the work were affected?
Evidence to request
  • Version history
  • Drafts
  • Disclosure statement
  • Conference record
Documentation note

Treat hidden AI use as a serious academic integrity concern. Follow school procedures.

13

AI generates an essay structure template

✓ Generally Acceptable (if acknowledged)

The student used AI to generate a template or structure for the essay.

Why it matters

A structure can support organisation, but should not determine the student's thinking, argument, or content.

Ask the student
  • How did the template influence your essay?
  • What did you adapt?
  • What is your own argument?
  • Did you disclose this?
Evidence to request
  • AI-generated template
  • Student outline
  • Draft development
  • Disclosure statement
Documentation note

Generally not misconduct if acknowledged. Students must still create their own argument, research, and writing.

Teacher Authenticity Checklist

Is this genuinely the student's own work?

This is not an AI detection checklist. It helps teachers evaluate authenticity through professional judgement, process evidence, and student explanation.

Professional judgement

The aim is not to prove that AI wrote the work. The aim is to make a reasonable professional judgement about whether the submitted work reflects the student's own understanding, analysis, voice, process, and decision-making.

How to use the checklist

Tick an item when you have reviewed it, not to mean “yes” or “authentic”. Questions include both positive evidence and potential concerns. This completion bar is not an authenticity score. Record findings in Template 4. Consider agreed access arrangements, language background, anxiety, and work completed offline before interpreting differences or gaps.

Teacher viva questions — click to refresh

What to do when there are concerns

  1. Do not rely only on AI detection tools
  2. Review the student's drafts and developmental work
  3. Review NoodleTools notes, citations, and research trail
  4. Ask the student to explain the work orally
  5. Ask for AI documentation if AI use is disclosed or suspected
  6. Compare the submitted work with known student work
  7. Ask the student to locate and explain evidence in cited sources
  8. Consult the school's academic integrity policy
  9. Record the concern and the evidence reviewed
  10. If authenticity cannot be confirmed, follow school procedures
  11. Where appropriate, ask the student to redo the task in controlled conditions
The professional question is not "Can I prove AI wrote this?" The better question is "Do I have enough evidence to authenticate this as the student's own work?"
Assessment and Coursework

Applying the framework to coursework

Subject-specific guidance for the Extended Essay, Internal Assessments, written coursework, and research tasks.

EE

Extended Essay

Clarify AI use for brainstorming vs writing. Agree suitable research records; NoodleTools is one option, not an IB requirement. Students explain how they developed their question through supervisor dialogue. Any permitted AI examples are starting points for discussion, not a substitute for their own question development.

IA

Internal Assessments

Subject-specific AI expectations must be clear. Show process evidence and use oral check-ins to confirm understanding. Transparent documentation required where AI was used.

WC

Written Coursework

Specify permitted planning support and basic non-generative spelling/grammar checks, where allowed. AI must not replace the student’s assessed writing. Submit drafts and reflection alongside final work.

OT

Presentations and Oral Tasks

Define permitted preparation support under the subject’s rules. Students explain content in their own words. Oral questioning confirms understanding and source knowledge.

RA

Research-Based Assignments

Teach SIFT and lateral reading. Require triangulation of key claims. Use NoodleTools for research organisation. Distinguish AI suggestions from verified evidence.

Key messages for teachers

Do not assume students know how to use AI responsibly. Teach it explicitly.
Use information literacy as the foundation for AI literacy.
Make process evidence visible from day one — the process is part of the proof.
Normalise honest documentation. Treat disclosure as academic integrity.
Set up shared documents and version-tracked workspaces from the start of every major task.
Prioritise dialogue, evidence, and reflection over detection alone.
Adapt these ideas to help make student thinking and AI support more visible.
